### Fretboard recognition
Get better at taking a major or minor triad (using a set of 3 strings) and figuring out where all the other chords are (ie find its iii, III, ii, V)
- do this for all stringsets (GBe, DGB, etc)

Get better at starting a scale starting in one position and ending on another
- e.g. starting on a low C in centre shape and then ending on right shape

Work on DGB minor sets, inc vi-ii-iii and vi-ii-III
Work on connecting those minor sets to their corresponding shapes on the GBe strings
Work on connecting the minor scale degrees to the major ones (ie be able to play all I ii ii IV V vi)

Work on stringsets that are not in order of 1-3-5 bass (usually involves skipping a string)
- ex. 3-1-5 (basically do a diminished chord and raise the b5 a whole step)
- ex. 1-5-3 (this is basically a bar chord stripped to its basic 3 notes)
- ex. 5-3-1

Get better at being able to play the roots alone in any progression
- ex. do the 1-4-5 movement on the G-B-e and D-G-B stringsets moving up the fretboard, but just hit the roots.
- The purpose of this exercise is to bring everything back to the visual intervals on the fretboard. Trust that our fingers know how to make the chord shape for any given root position. For instance when on the G-B-e stringset and are targetting the B-string for our root note, then we know we must make a 5-bass chord. With this practice, we'll get to a place where we can see the root position, and build a chord around it.
- Added benefit: 1-4-5 movement is identical to 6-2-3 movement.

Get better at recognizing intervals across multiple octaves
- ex. take a 3rd and go up a fourth interval. What interval (compared to the root) is this? (answer: 6th). Go up another fourth. Now what interval is this? (answer: 2nd)
- to practice, pick an interval at random (e.g. 2nd) and ask, "if this is 2nd, what is this one?". Use the fourth movements to anchor.

Get better at recognizing intervals that are lower in pitch to the root
- we're sitting pretty good on intervals above, but need to work on below the root in pitch, esp around higher strings

Get better at taking any note on the fretboard and saying "if this note is the 6th, where is the 2?"
- this will help us learn the shapes of intervals and be able to navigate better. If we only practice "if this is the 1, then this is the 6th", then we aren't able to navigate to other chords. Think, it's easy to answer the question "if this is the 3, where is the 4?". That's always a semitone. Super straightforward. We need to be able to do the same thing for other intervals.
- The pattern of going down a 4th is: `1 4 b7 b3 b6 b2 b5 7 3 6 2 5` (essentially circle of fifths going counterclockwise). Notice that the 73625 pattern is repeated. Internalize the pattern, and be able to enter the chain at any point.

get better at seeing intervals on open strings

While doing chords and making modifications, focus on one modification at a time. Go through the 1-4-5 exercises, but instead of modifying every interval, just add one. Focus on the 2. Play each chord inversion, and play the 2

Know how to move an octave on neighboring strings. Bonus here is that if you can get to the octave quickly, you can get to the 5th easily (since octave and 5th are at same fret)
- *octave up* - go up a string, and move up frets equal to a major 3rd plus a minor 3rd

Know how to find unison between neighboring strings.
- *unison moving to lower string* - go down a string, and move up a 4th
- *unison moving to higher string* - go up a string, and move down a 4th

### Being aware
- notice that augmented shapes are identical in each inversion. Therefore, each augmented chord is also 2 other augmented chords, since each chord tone can be considered as the root (and each note can be considered the major 3rd and each can be considered the #5). Therefore, notice that if we play any augmented chord and flatten ANY one of the notes, we will be playing a major chord.
    - also notice that we can derive the shapes of the major triads this way (and across each stringset), just by treating each note of the augmented chord as its #5 and flattening it
    - also notice that if you take an augmented chord and flatten the 3rd, you get another major chord (an alternative way to see this: take a major tried (`1-3-5`) and push apart the 3 and the 5 (`1-b3-#5`))

### Come up with 4 note chords quickly
Technique (Major 6th chord as example):
1. Identify which bass is closest to the added tone (in this case, 5, since 6 is just a whole tone up from 5)
2. Play a 4-note 5-bass chord
3. Since you have two distinct 5th intervals, shift one of them up a whole tone
