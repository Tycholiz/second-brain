Microphone Placement

Stop thinking of mic placement as "aim at the source, get more source." Instead think of it as choosing which part of the sound field you want the mic to capture.

Close Miking

When you strike a drum, two things happen almost simultaneously but not quite:

  1. The initial impact — skin stretching, the stick or hand contact. This is percussive, sharp, and loud. It happens in milliseconds.
  2. The drum body resonating — the air inside the drum moving, the frame vibrating, the skin oscillating back and forth after the hit. This takes slightly longer to develop and decays over time.

These two events travel outward as sound waves in all directions.

What a close mic hears:

  • It's so near the source that it essentially catches event 1 almost exclusively. The resonance is still developing when the mic is already capturing it at full volume. The result is a waveform that looks like a sharp spike and then falls off quickly — all attack, minimal sustain. That's the "dead" sound. It's not actually dead, it's just that you've captured the least interesting part of the sound in disproportionate detail.

What a mic a foot away hears:

  • By the time the sound wave travels that foot, event 1 has already partially dissipated and event 2 — the resonance and body — has had time to develop and join it. The mic hears a more complete picture of the instrument's full character. The waveform has a fatter initial transient and a longer, more gradual tail.

close miking feels like you're getting more of the instrument, because you're closer to it. But you're actually getting less of it, because you're only capturing the attack and missing the resonance that gives it character and identity. Pulling back feels like you're getting less, but you're actually getting more of what makes the instrument sound like itself.

Proximity effect

The closer a mic to its sound source, the more low end will be registered

The more on-axis a mic is (ie. the more directly it’s pointed at the source), the brighter it will sound

Note: this is a characteristic of directional mics — cardioid, supercardioid, figure-8 — where bass frequencies are boosted as the mic gets closer to the source. It’s a physical property of how directional mics work, not a property of the sound itself. Omnidirectional mics don’t exhibit proximity effect at all.
